Drawing the Line on AI in a Dental Practice

How a healthcare practice adopted AI tools and smart devices without exposing itself to legal liability.

The client

A private dental practice. Like most healthcare practices, it operates under strict patient privacy obligations, including HIPAA, and its clinical spaces are among the most privacy-sensitive environments in any small business.

(Anonymized at the client's request.)

The problem

Unmanaged risk, one device at a time

Staff had started using AI tools and smart devices in and around the practice: smart glasses, wearable recorders, phone cameras, and AI-powered apps. Each use seemed harmless in isolation. Together, they created unmanaged risk:

  • No rules governed when recording or photography was permitted, by whom, or of what.
  • No policy addressed AI tools processing information that could include patient data.
  • The practice had no documented position to point to if a patient complained, a device captured something it should not have, or a regulator asked questions.

The practice did not want to ban the technology. It wanted to use it safely. That distinction is the entire engagement: the goal was a clear boundary, not a blanket prohibition.

The engagement

How I drew the line

I was brought in to define that boundary. The work followed the same process I use for every operations engagement:

  1. Assess.

    I reviewed how staff were actually using devices and AI tools day to day, not how anyone assumed they were being used. The gap between the two is where liability lives.

  2. Identify the exposure.

    I mapped the specific scenarios that created legal risk: recording in clinical areas, patient-identifiable data reaching third-party AI services, consent gaps, and staff personal devices.

  3. Draw the lines.

    I drafted a recording, photography, AI, and smart device policy that stated, in plain language, what staff may do, what they may not, and what requires patient consent. No ambiguity, no legalese staff would ignore.

  4. Make it operational.

    A policy only works if people follow it. The document was written for daily use by front-office and clinical staff, not for a binder.

The outcome

The result

  • The practice adopted the policy without disruption to operations or staff pushback.
  • Staff now have clear, written rules for recording, photography, and AI tool use, including where patient consent is required.
  • The practice has a documented, defensible position on AI and smart device use, closing a liability gap that previously had no answer.
  • Technology use continued. The practice drew a line; it did not build a wall.
